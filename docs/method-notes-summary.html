3D Wind Rose · In brief

Method notes in brief

The short version: what the tool counts, and how far a number off the rose can be carried. It bins recorded climate data into a 16-direction frequency table and draws it. No building enters that calculation — loading a different model does not change a single frequency, calm percentage or monthly figure. The full method notes set out how the weather file is read, where the presets come from, and the provenance of every figure in full.

Audience Architecture students and staff Method EPW frequency binning · no flow solved Runs Entirely in the browser Version 1.0.1 · 17 September 2026

Three things to know before you cite a number

  1. The rose is the weather station’s climate, not the site’s wind. EPW wind speed is a 10 m open-country measurement taken at the station. No height, terrain or roughness correction, and no displacement adjustment, exists anywhere in the code. So the rose describes the regional climate the station recorded, not the wind arriving at a particular building’s façade. Use it to compare directions and seasons, not to read off a design wind speed.
  2. Calm hours are counted, but they are not drawn. An hour with a wind speed below 0.5 m/s is recorded as calm and given no direction, so it appears in no petal. It is still counted in the yearly total that every percentage is worked out against. So the petals cannot add up to the whole year: add up every petal on a rose built from a file that is 8.3% calm, and you get 91.7%. The missing 8.3% is the calm hours — counted, but drawn nowhere. Always quote the calm percentage next to any figure you read off the petals, or your numbers will not add up for anyone checking them.
  3. The wind data and the map location can be two different places — and “Sheffield” is Doncaster Airport. The built-in Sheffield preset is recorded at Doncaster Sheffield Airport (WMO 034054), about 32 km from the city centre and in a different landscape: the airport sits in the lowland Don valley at roughly 9 m, the city centre at roughly 60 m with the Peak District immediately west. The tool’s own label says so — “Sheffield (Doncaster Airport)” — but the parenthesis is easy to read past. More generally, the tool tracks the gap between where the loaded wind data came from and wherever the map is centred, and warns once that gap passes 50 km.

What it computes

A frequency table, binned once from whichever EPW records are loaded, drawn as a stack of coloured wedges. Nothing else. Sixteen direction sectors of 22.5° each, five speed bins — 0–2, 2–4, 4–6, 6–8 and an open-ended 8+ m/s — and one percentage per cell, count divided by total records. Records below 0.5 m/s are calm and sit outside every sector.

Sector assignment uses Math.round(direction ÷ 22.5) mod 16, not Math.floor. That matters when you read a petal: sector 0 spans 348.75°–11.25°, centred on due north, rather than running from 0° to 22.5°. Every sector is centred on its compass point the same way.

Months are grouped by the EPW file’s own month field and each group is binned independently, so a month’s percentages are a share of that month’s own hours, not of the year. Alongside the rose, the sidebar reports the total hours behind whichever table is displayed and that table’s calm percentage — both taken from the same count that builds the rose, not worked out separately.

Summed across all 16 sectors and 5 bins, the petals add up to 100 minus the calm percentage. That is not a rounding error — it is the calm hours, counted in the total but drawn in no direction. See the second trap.

The building is not in the calculation

This is the single most important thing to understand about the tool, and the easiest to forget, because a building is sitting right there on screen next to the rose. The two files that produce every number the rose shows — js/epw.js and js/windrose.js — take a plain list of {direction, speed, month} records and nothing else. Neither file contains any reference to a building, a model or a footprint. Loading a different .obj changes how large the rose is drawn, and nothing about what it says.

What follows from that is a list of things a general wind-analysis package might offer and this tool does not:

Out
Any influence of the building or its surroundings
No downwash, channelling, wake or shelter. The rose has no idea a building is present.
Out
Height correction
EPW wind speed is measured at 10 m in open country at the station. No code anywhere adjusts it to any other height.
Out
Terrain and roughness
No roughness length, terrain category or displacement height appears anywhere in the source.
Out
Gust factors and exceedance statistics
Mean hourly frequencies only. No gust-equivalent speed, no exceedance probability of any kind.
Out
Weibull or any distribution fitting
The rose is a direct histogram of recorded hours. No distribution is fitted to it.
Out
Comfort criteria of any kind
No comfort classes are assigned — unlike the sister tool, Urban Wind Lab, which applies (and heavily caveats) Lawson bands. A wind rose alone, with no flow field and no correction to the wind actually reaching a building, cannot support a comfort claim.

The question is not precision, it is representativeness

Unlike a solver, there is no approximation in the arithmetic: a frequency is a count divided by a total, computed once and read straight into the rose. So the honest question is a different one — what a correctly counted number from this particular source can and cannot tell you. Four things limit that, independently of each other.

The source year. A TMYx file is a synthesised representative year, assembled by picking the most statistically representative version of each calendar month from many years at that station and stitching the twelve together. No date inside it corresponds to weather that actually occurred on that date. The 22.5° sector. Two real peaks 15° apart are drawn as one. The five bins, with an open top. Nothing distinguishes a 9 m/s hour from a 20 m/s hour — both fall in “8+”. The station itself. A 10 m open-country reading, uncorrected, as above.

The rose’s drawn size is not data

The rose’s inner radius, its overall span and even its petal thickness are all set from the loaded model’s footprint — or from a fixed default footprint when nothing is loaded — and the whole drawing is then rescaled so the tallest direction’s stack always reaches the same span. Two roses are therefore never comparable by their size on screen: a small-looking rose over a large building and a large-looking rose over a small one can show identical wind climates. Only the proportions within one rose — which direction is tallest, how the bins stack — carry any information at all.

What you may and may not quote

  • May: the prevailing direction, and how the petal proportions change between two months or two locations.
  • May: the calm percentage — and quote it alongside any petal reading, not instead of one.
  • May not: a wind speed at a building, read off the rose as if it were measured there. The source never leaves the weather station.
  • May not: a comfort or shelter claim of any kind. The tool computes none.
  • May not: the rose’s size on screen, as evidence of anything. It is auto-scaled to the building’s footprint, not to the data.

Three UK locations are built in, all binned from real recorded TMYx EPW files of 8,760 hourly observations each, with a genuine month-by-month breakdown: London (St James’s Park, WMO 037700), Sheffield (Doncaster Airport, WMO 034054) and Edinburgh (Turnhouse Airport, WMO 031600). Name the station, not the city, when you report a figure.

Three exercises

Each teaches one principle using only the built-in presets — no upload needed — and takes ten to fifteen minutes. The diagrams beside them are schematic: they show the mechanism, not the output of any particular run.

Exercise 01

The prevailing direction moves with the seasons

  1. Load the London preset.
  2. Set the Month slider to January and note which direction has the tallest stack.
  3. Move it to July and note the tallest direction again.
  4. Switch back to All Year and compare both months against the annual shape.

Principle. The annual rose is an average of twelve different climates, and no single month necessarily resembles it. A façade opened for summer ventilation on the annual prevailing direction may face entirely the wrong way for infiltration and heat loss in January. Read the month you actually care about, not just “All Year”.

Exercise 02

A rose cannot tell you where a building is sheltered

  1. Load any preset, then import an .obj model — or leave the default placeholder footprint in place.
  2. Note the rose’s shape and size.
  3. Change the Scale slider from 100% to 250%, then back down to 50%.
  4. Watch the rose while you do it.

Principle. The rose’s size changes with the building’s footprint; its shape — the only part that carries data — does not move at all, because nothing about the geometry enters the binning. A rose has no idea a building is even present, so it cannot show downwash, channelling, wake or shelter of any kind. Reading those needs a flow tool — this tool’s own sister application, Urban Wind Lab, solves exactly that, on the explicit condition of accepting its own, quite different, set of caveats.

Exercise 03

How often is not how hard

  1. Load the Edinburgh preset, All Year.
  2. Find the longest petal — WSW, at 24.7% of the year. Note how much of it is in the top two colours, the 6–8 and 8+ bins: 44% of its hours.
  3. Now look at SW. It blows half as often — 12.1% — but the same share of its hours is that strong: 45%.
  4. Now look at ENE, at 6.2% of the year. Only 17% of its hours reach 6 m/s. Check NE (15%) and E (12%) as well.
  5. Write the directions out against two numbers each: how often it blows, and what share of that is at 6 m/s or more. The two do not rank the same way.

Principle. A direction has two properties and the rose shows both — how often it blows, as the length of the petal, and how hard, as the colour along it. In Edinburgh they part company by compass quarter: the westerlies blow often and hard, while the easterlies blow a fair amount and almost never hard, with a third as much of their time above 6 m/s. Wind’s momentum scales with the square of its speed, so for structural loading or driving rain the share in the top bins can matter more than the length of the petal. Read the colour, not only how far it reaches.

Where it stops

The tool is built to teach what a wind rose is and how one is assembled from recorded data. For a wind statement that must inform a real design decision, correct the data for height and terrain first, and bring in a flow tool or a wind engineer for anything involving a building’s response to that wind.

Two limitations of the artefact itself are worth stating plainly. It is unvalidated: no comparison against a published wind rose for the same station exists in the tool or anywhere behind it, and no benchmark or reference dataset appears anywhere in the source. And it is not self-contained: a MapTiler API key sits as a plain string in js/main.js, visible to anyone who views the source, and the running tool requests map tiles and location search from MapTiler over the network. It does still work offline — after six seconds without a loaded map style the code falls back to a blank, valid style, and the building and rose render fully; only the imagery underneath is missing.
